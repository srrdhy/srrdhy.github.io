Parametric PINN: One Moving Color Band, a Whole Family of Solutions

Created2026-11-04|Updated2026-11-04|PINN
|Post Views:

A conveyor belt whose ends meet, one meter in circumference, its surface mottled with light and dark patches. This minimal example treats the wave speed $c$ in the transport equation as an input to the network too: train once, and you can query the solution at any wave speed, instead of training a separate network for each $c$. The periodic boundary is hard-constrained by a coordinate mapping, which spares us an extra penalty term.

The Governing Equation

Take the one-dimensional transport equation:

$$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+c \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=0. $$

Its initial condition is $u(x,0)=u_0(x)=[1+\sin(2\pi x)]/2$, and the periodic boundary is $u(0,t)=u(1,t)$.

Take $0\le x\le 1.0$, $0\le t\le 0.5$, $0.2\le c\le 1.0$. The reference solution for this family of problems can be worked out by hand: $u_c(x,t)=u_0(x-ct)$ — the color pattern simply translates along with the belt, its shape unchanged.

The point of this example is feeding $c$ into the network: $x,t,c$ are all inputs, and the network learns the entire family of solutions $u(x,t;c)$, not merely a single “film”.

Network Construction

Map the one-dimensional spatial coordinate $x\in[0,1]$ onto the two-dimensional unit circle: $x \mapsto \big(\cos(2\pi x), \sin(2\pi x)\big)$, thereby building a hard-constrained periodic boundary condition (Hard-constrained Periodic BC):

$$ \cos(2\pi \times 0) = \cos(2\pi \times 1) = 1, \quad \sin(2\pi \times 0) = \sin(2\pi \times 1) = 0 $$

The input features coincide exactly at $x=0$ and $x=1$, so the network output satisfies periodicity strictly by construction:

$$ \hat{u}(0, t, c) \equiv \hat{u}(1, t, c) $$

This removes the need to add an extra periodic-boundary penalty term to the loss.

Time variable normalization: 4 * t - 1, mapping $t\in[0,0.5]$ linearly onto $[-1,1]$.

Parametrized transport speed normalization: (c - 0.6) / 0.4, centering $c\in[0.2,1.0]$ and scaling it onto $[-1,1]$ before feeding it to the network as an input feature.

The four feature channels are therefore:

$$ \mathbf{X}_{\text{in}} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(2\pi x) & \sin(2\pi x) & \tilde{t} & \tilde{c} \end{bmatrix} $$

All four feature channels are squeezed into $[-1,1]$, the near-linear region where the derivative of the Tanh activation is largest and the unit is most sensitive, which keeps neurons from entering the flat saturation region too early and causing vanishing gradients.

Network Training

At each step, $x, t, c$ are drawn at random from a uniform distribution, the PDE residual is computed by automatic differentiation, and then a separate batch of points constrains the initial pattern at $t=0$, with the initial loss given 4 times the weight:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \left(\hat{u}_t + c \hat{u}_x\right)^2}_{\text{equation error}} + 4 \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_j \left(\hat{u}(x_j,0,c_j)-\frac{1+\sin(2\pi x_j)}{2}\right)^2}_{\text{initial error}} $$

1 3.10e-02 2.04e-01

5000 8.64e-04 5.88e-05

Why the Fixed Grid Is Worse: Empirical Risk and True Risk

The ultimate goal of solving a PDE with a PINN is to minimize the expected risk over the entire continuous domain:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{true}} = \int_{\Omega} \left\vert{} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + c \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right\vert{}^2 d\Omega = \mathbb{E}_{(x,t,c) \sim \mathcal{U}(\Omega)} \left[ \mathcal{R}^2(x,t,c) \right] $$

  • A fixed grid builds the empirical risk from only a very small number of points ($N=128$):

    $$ \hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{grid}} = \frac{1}{128} \sum_{i=1}^{128} \mathcal{R}^2(x_i^{\text{grid}}, t_i^{\text{grid}}, c_i^{\text{grid}}) $$

    The optimizer can drive $\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{grid}}$ extremely low, but because the quadrature weights and sampling density are insufficient, this empirical value has already drifted far away from the true integral $\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}$.

  • Dynamic random sampling is a form of stochastic approximation: each iteration uses an independent Monte Carlo mini-batch, and the gradient updates push the model toward the minimizer of the continuous integral over the whole domain. Although the random fluctuation of a single batch keeps the printed loss hovering around $10^{-4}$, what the network learns is a robust approximation of the entire continuous solution manifold, and its corresponding $\mathcal{L}_{\text{true}}$ is far lower than that of the fixed-grid model.

Summary

  • Treating the physical parameter $c$ as an input feature lets a single network cover the whole family of solutions; normalizing to $[-1,1]$ keeps Tanh working in its sensitive range.
  • Encoding the periodic boundary into the inputs via $x \mapsto (\cos 2\pi x, \sin 2\pi x)$ makes the boundary condition hold by construction, with no penalty term needed.
  • A low training loss does not mean high accuracy: fixed collocation lets the network “memorize” those points, whereas random sampling is what approximates the expected risk over the continuous domain.
