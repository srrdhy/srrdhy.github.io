FB-PINN Paper Reading 01 - Normalization and Hard Constraints

|Post Views:

Note

For a PINN, the loss usually consists of two basic terms, the physics loss and the boundary loss:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_p(\theta) + \mathcal{L}_b(\theta),$$

The two terms take the following forms:

$$\mathcal{L}_p(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i}^{N_p} \| \mathcal{D}[NN(x_i; \theta); \lambda] - f(x_i) \|^2,$$ $$\mathcal{L}_b(\theta) = \sum_{k} \frac{1}{N_{bk}} \sum_{j}^{N_{bk}} \| \mathcal{B}_k[NN(x_{kj}; \theta)] - g_k(x_{kj}) \|^2,$$

The form above is called a soft constraint. In practice the two terms often conflict during training, and tuning their weighting coefficients is also tedious work. To address this, the boundary conditions can be built directly into the approximate solution as a part that is necessarily satisfied, for example for the wave equation:

$$\left[ \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} \right] u(x, t) = f(x, t),$$ $$u(x, 0) = g_1(x),$$ $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x, 0) = g_2(x),$$

we can assume the approximate solution takes the form:

$$\hat{u}(x, t; \theta) = g_1(x) + t\,g_2(x) + t^2\,NN(x, t; \theta)$$

In this way, at $t=0$ the solution is forced to satisfy $g_1(x)$, and after differentiating with respect to $t$, at $t=0$ it is also forced to satisfy $g_2(x)$; the loss therefore only needs to contain the physics loss $\mathcal{L}_p(\theta)$, turning the problem into a simple unconstrained optimization.

Consider a one-dimensional example:
$$\frac{du}{dx} = \cos(\omega x),$$ $$u(0) = 0,$$
This equation has the exact solution:
$$u(x) = \frac{1}{\omega} \sin(\omega x)$$
We design the approximate solution as:
$$\hat{u}(x; \theta) = \tanh(\omega x) NN(x; \theta)$$
Here at $x=0$, $tanh(\omega x)=0$, and away from the boundary $x=0$ its value tends to $\pm 1$, so the neural network does not need to compensate for that function extensively. The loss function finally used by the PINN is:
$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_p(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i}^{N_p} \left\| \frac{d}{dx} \hat{u}(x_i; \theta) - \cos(\omega x_i) \right\|^2$$
We first train on the low-frequency case $\omega = 1$, using a fully connected network with 2 hidden layers of 16 neurons each and tanh activation, with 200 training points uniformly distributed over $x \in [-2\pi, 2\pi]$. Before $x$ enters the network it is normalized to $[-1, 1]$, and to match the amplitude range of the exact solution the output is multiplied by $\frac{1}{\omega}$.

In the high-frequency case $\omega = 15$, the training points are increased to 3000, and three different network configurations are used; the resulting predictions and loss decay are shown below.

Although the PINN performs well at low frequency, it faces great difficulty at high frequency: learning high frequencies requires a large number of parameters, converges slowly, and has low accuracy. For this problem, extending to higher frequency is equivalent to extending to a larger domain size, because we already normalize $x$ to $[-1, 1]$ before feeding it into the network, so keeping $\omega = 1$ fixed while enlarging the domain by a factor of 15 and renormalizing is the same for optimization as setting $\omega = 15$ directly. This leads to FB-PINN. The figure below compares the $L1$ errors of the four cases.
